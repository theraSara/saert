# SparseRT — Project Notes

**What internal computations of GPT-2 predict when humans slow down while reading?**

This project bridges psycholinguistics and mechanistic interpretability. 
We use Sparse Autoencoders to open up a language model's hidden states
and ask which of its internal features predict human reading difficulty
better than surprisal. The answer turns out to be much richer than
surprisal alone captures — and the features that matter are completely
different depending on how you measure reading time.

---

At the feature interpretation stage. The regression pipeline is
complete, results are solid and replicated across two corpora, and now
there's three independent methods of characterising what the predictive
features represent linguistically. The main result is publishable.
The two open threads are: (1) formal write-up of the L01 NS exclusion,
and (2) participant-level robustness check for Natural Stories.

---

## The research question

Language models like GPT-2 are routinely used to predict human reading
times via **surprisal** — how unexpected is word W given everything before
it? Surprisal is a scalar: it collapses the model's entire internal
computation into a single number, discarding most of the structure.

We ask: is the structure that gets discarded what humans are actually
sensitive to? If so, which specific internal features encode that
structure, and what do they represent linguistically?

**Why this is hard to answer with standard probing.**
Most probing approaches train a new classifier on the model's hidden
states to predict some external label. This tells you what information
is present, but not what the model is computing or why. We take a
mechanistic interpretability approach instead — we decompose the hidden
state into a sparse set of pre-trained features using a Sparse Autoencoder
(SAE), so the features are interpretable before we ever look at reading
times. Then we ask which features the regression selects.

---

## Corpora

We use two reading time datasets with different measurement paradigms.
This was deliberate — if a finding replicates across both, it is more
likely to reflect genuine cognitive processing rather than measurement
artefacts.

| Corpus | Measure | Words | Texts | Mean RT |
|--------|---------|-------|-------|---------|
| **Provo** | First-fixation duration (FFD) | 2,743 | 55 | ~208 ms |
| **Natural Stories** | Self-paced reading (SPR) | 10,256 | 10 | ~340 ms |

**Why FFD and SPR differ.**
FFD reflects the first uninterrupted gaze on a word — typically 150–250 ms.
At this timescale, processing is mostly automatic: the reader is accessing
the word's form, retrieving its meaning, and checking basic fit with the
preceding context. SPR is a button-press task where readers control when
the next word appears. At 300–400 ms, readers have time for deliberate
syntactic parsing, discourse model updating, and reference resolution.
These two measures are sensitive to different levels of processing — a
finding that the layer-selection results directly confirm.

## Key design decisions

### Why this SAE?

We use `gpt2-small-res-jb` (Bloom 2024), a set of 13 SAEs (one per
residual stream layer) trained on GPT-2 small using the standard
Anthropic recipe. Each SAE has 24,576 features — 32× the model's
hidden dimension of 768. The sparsity constraint (TopK, k≈20–80 per
word) means only a handful of features fire on any given word, making
the decomposition interpretable without further work.

We chose this SAE rather than, say, training our own because: (a) it is
publicly available and widely used in interpretability work, making our
results comparable with the literature; (b) it is already validated on
GPT-2 small specifically; (c) training a good SAE requires careful
hyperparameter tuning that is orthogonal to our research question.

### Why ridge regression, not Lasso or OLS?

The regression predicts reading time from SAE feature activations. We
have up to 24,576 features and only 2,743 words (Provo). OLS is
underdetermined. Lasso would force exactly-zero coefficients, which
makes sense if we believe only a handful of features matter, but the
SAE already enforces sparsity at the representation level — applying
sparsity twice (in the SAE encoding and again in the regression) would
be excessively restrictive. Ridge penalises large coefficients smoothly,
selects a regularisation strength via inner cross-validation, and
remains stable under collinearity between features.

### Why leave-one-text-out?

Standard k-fold cross-validation treats each word as an independent
observation. But words within a text share context and are correlated.
Leave-one-text-out holds out all words from one text at a time, so
the test words genuinely come from a different context than the training
words. This is more conservative but more realistic.

### Why two hook types (prefix and post)?

Every hook extracts two hidden states per word:
- **Prefix (before-word)**: the model's state at word position N using
  only left-context tokens. This captures what the model *anticipates*
  before seeing the word.
- **Post (after-word)**: the model's state after the word has been
  integrated. This captures how the model *represents* the word in context.

Comparing prefix and post coefficients for the same feature reveals
whether the reading time effect is anticipatory (reader was surprised
by the word) or integrative (reader struggled to fit the word into
the sentence). This distinction would be invisible with surprisal alone.

### Why surprisal with spillover lags?

A substantial body of psycholinguistic research shows that reading
difficulty on word N often shows up in reading time at N+1 or N+2 —
readers do not always slow down on the difficult word itself. We include
surprisal at the current word and two previous words as controls. This
makes the baseline more competitive and ensures that any additional
variance explained by SAE features is genuinely new signal, not
spillover effects we forgot to account for.

### Why logit lens + Neuronpedia + SRP for interpretation?

These three methods are complementary and independent.

**Logit lens** applies GPT-2's own unembedding matrix to each SAE
feature's decoder vector to produce a ranked list of predicted vocabulary
tokens. It is fast, automatic, and uses only model weights — but it is
corpus-dependent (the tokens you see depend partly on which text was used
to fit the lens).

**Neuronpedia** provides GPT-4-generated descriptions obtained by showing
the top-activating examples from a large corpus to GPT-4 and asking it
to describe the feature. This is the most human-readable description but
requires access to the external Neuronpedia API and depends on the
quality of GPT-4's interpretation.

**Sparse Readout Prism (SRP)** (He et al. 2026) decomposes the logit
lens output into sparse readout features — directions in the unembedding
matrix trained to be reusable and corpus-independent. It gives a
fidelity diagnostic (what fraction of the logit is explained by the sparse
approximation), and reveals when two SAE features share the same underlying
readout direction. We trained SRP on GPT-2 small's W_U (no pretrained
checkpoint existed), achieving rowEV = 0.80 — adequate for interpretation.

Using all three gives triangulated evidence: we trust a label most when
the logit lens tokens, the Neuronpedia description, and the dominant SRP
readout direction all point to the same linguistic phenomenon.

## Results

### 1. Surprisal baseline

Surprisal explains a statistically significant but small fraction of
reading time variance, above lexical controls (word frequency, length,
sentence position) and spillover lags.

| Corpus | Context | ΔR² | 95% CI |
|--------|---------|-----|--------|
| Provo FFD | Both contexts (identical) | **+1.48 pp** | [0.54, 2.35] |
| NS SPR | 1,024-token + spillover | **+4.38 pp** | [2.34, 6.34] |
| NS SPR | 128-token + spillover | **+4.16 pp** | [1.80, 6.00] |

Surprisal explains more variance in SPR than in FFD. This is consistent
with the literature: self-paced reading is a more deliberate, prediction-
sensitive measure. The effect is real but small — there is clearly much
more information in the model's internal states that surprisal is not
capturing.

### 2. SAE feature gain above the surprisal baseline

| Corpus | Hook type | SAE gain | 95% CI | Texts improved |
|--------|-----------|----------|--------|----------------|
| Provo FFD | Before word (prefix) | **+2.87 pp** | [+1.77, +3.98] | 43/55 |
| Provo FFD | After word (post) | **+6.58 pp** | [+5.42, +7.78] | **50/55** |
| NS SPR | Before word (prefix) | **+0.82 pp** | [+0.19, +1.37] | 7/10 |
| NS SPR | After word (post) | **+3.84 pp** | [+2.35, +5.18] | **10/10** |

SAE features explain 2–6× more variance than surprisal above the baseline.
The after-word hook is always more predictive than the before-word hook.
This tells us that it is the model's *integration* of the target word —
not its anticipatory state — that contains most of the RT-relevant signal.

### 3. SAE versus dense hidden states

This is the most theoretically interesting comparison. The question is:
does the SAE's sparsity constraint help or hurt, relative to using the
full 768-dimensional hidden state directly?

| Corpus | Hook type | SAE − dense | 95% CI | Verdict |
|--------|-----------|-------------|--------|---------|
| Provo FFD | Before word | **+1.80 pp** | [+0.67, +2.88] | SAE better |
| Provo FFD | After word | **+3.78 pp** | [+2.73, +4.88] | SAE better |
| NS SPR | Before word | −1.79 pp | [−3.49, +0.25] | Not significant |
| NS SPR | After word | **−2.39 pp** | [−4.02, −0.45] | Dense better |

**For Provo (eye-tracking), sparse is better. For Natural Stories (SPR),
dense is better.** This dissociation is the main theoretical contribution
of the project.

Eye-tracking FFD at ~200 ms reflects fast lexical processing. A sparse
set of features encoding word identity and semantic surprise is sufficient
to predict it, and the SAE's sparsity constraint removes noise from
irrelevant features. SPR at ~340 ms reflects slower integrative processing
where the full contextual representation matters — forcing sparsity
discards information the SPR measure is sensitive to.

### 4. Layer selection

The inner cross-validation selects which layer's hidden state to use per
outer fold. 

| Corpus | Hook | Representation | Selected layer | Folds |
|--------|------|----------------|----------------|-------|
| Provo | post | SAE | **L01** | 55/55 |
| Provo | prefix | SAE | **L01** | 55/55 |
| NS | post | SAE | **L08** | 5/10 |
| NS | prefix | SAE | **L08** | 4/10 |

Provo universally selects L01 — the embedding layer, before any attention
has run. Natural Stories selects L08 — after 8 rounds of contextual
processing. This is not a coincidence. FFD reflects early lexical access
that depends on word identity (encoded in the embedding), while SPR
reflects integrative processing that depends on context (encoded only
after multiple attention layers have operated).

The layer dissociation mirrors the measure dissociation exactly. Same
model, same text, different RT measure → different optimal layer.

## Feature interpretation

### Methods

Three interpretation methods applied to the top-30 features at each
of the four (corpus × hook type) combinations:

1. **Logit lens** — `W_unembed @ LayerNorm(W_dec[:, i])` → top-20 tokens
2. **Neuronpedia** — GPT-4 auto-interp via public API (`/api/feature/gpt2-small/{layer}-res-jb/{id}`)
3. **SRP** — GPT-2-specific SRP trained on W_U (rowEV = 0.80, k = 64, d_features = 4096)

### Category profile (from Neuronpedia labels)

**Provo L01 post (after-word, eye-tracking):**
33% lexical complexity, 23% proper nouns, 20% discourse structure

The features that slow eye fixations are primarily about word identity:
morphologically complex words, named entities requiring world knowledge,
abstract philosophical vocabulary, unusual semantic clusters. These are
all computations the model performs at the embedding layer before any
contextual attention — which is why L01 is selected.

**NS L08 post (after-word, SPR):**
33% boundary/structural, 27% discourse structure, 23% register

SPR is slowed by structural transitions (sentence endings, heading
markers, date formats) and discourse-level features (political figures,
philosophical debates, social issues). These require 8 rounds of
attention to encode — which is why L08 is selected.

**Provo L01 prefix (before-word, anticipatory):**
40% lexical complexity, 23% predictability

When the context predicts a lexically complex word is coming (morpheme
fragments, academic vocabulary), the next word is harder. When the
context strongly predicts a function word (determiners, manner adverbs),
the next word is easier. This is the classic predictability facilitation
effect encoded as a feature.

**NS L08 prefix (before-word, anticipatory):**
23% register, 23% proper nouns, 17% syntactic integration

Before seeing the word, a legal/regulatory or politically-charged context
predicts harder SPR reading. The syntactic integration features here
(legal frameworks, criminal terminology, legislative decisions) are
contexts that predict syntactically complex upcoming material.

### Highlighted features

**Feature 9237 — the sign reversal** *(boundary/structural)*
NP label: "text punctuations"
SRP fidelity: 0.50 (lowest in the dataset)

This feature has opposite signs in prefix and post hooks for Provo:
- Prefix: +0.9883 (anticipating a clause boundary → *harder*)
- Post: −0.4459 (being at a clause boundary → *easier*)

The low SRP fidelity tells us why: this feature works through
suppression/inhibition rather than by promoting specific token
predictions. The SRP cannot capture inhibitory features well in its
sparse positive-contribution framework — the large residual is meaningful.
The sign reversal maps directly onto the psycholinguistic wrap-up effect
(readers accelerate at clause boundaries) versus the clause-initiation
cost (readers slow down when starting a new clause).

**Feature 985 — single dominant direction** *(lexical complexity)*
NP label: "verbs for sudden, sharp, unexpected actions"
SRP fidelity: 0.999 (highest in the dataset)
Top SRP feature: SRP#4047 (+1.078)

Near-perfect fidelity and a single dominant SRP direction. This feature
collapses almost entirely onto one readout direction — the model has a
single clean unembedding dimension for sudden action verbs. When this
fires in Provo text, fixation time increases.

**Features 1706 and 13505 — shared SRP direction** *(boundary/structural, register)*
NP: "dates and events" / "reusing code across modules"
Both load on SRP#2456 as their top contribution.

Despite having different Neuronpedia descriptions and different SAE
feature IDs, these two features share the same dominant SRP readout
direction. This is corpus-independent evidence that they tap the same
structural dimension of GPT-2's output space — likely a formal/structured-
text direction. The SRP analysis reveals structure that neither the logit
lens tokens nor the Neuronpedia labels expose individually.

**Feature 13976 — predictability facilitation** *(predictability)*
NP label: "adverbs expressing emotion or state"
SRP fidelity: 0.931 — Top SRP: SRP#1344 (+0.448)
Coefficient: −0.3652 (active → *easier* to read)

When the context before a word activates this feature, the next word is
easier to read. The logit lens top token is `proudly`, and SRP#1344
is clearly a manner-adverb direction in the unembedding. When preceding
context predicts a manner adverb, readers process it faster. This is
the expectation-based facilitation effect (Levy 2008) made mechanistically
visible in a specific model feature.

## Known issue: L01 exclusion for Natural Stories

`blocks.0.hook_resid_pre` (L01) is the raw embedding output before any
attention. The SAE was trained on post-attention residual streams, so
encoding raw embeddings produces out-of-distribution activations. For
Natural Stories (long stories, sliding window), this caused extreme
prediction errors (max 112 log-ms against a typical RT range of 5–6 log-ms).

The inner cross-validation correctly detected this — L01 was never
selected in any of the 10 NS folds. Main results are unaffected.

Provo is unaffected because short texts fit in a single 1,024-token
pass with no windowing discontinuities.

**Formal treatment in the paper:** state that L01 was excluded from the NS
hook pool after the audit. Cite the scaling sensitivity analysis (notebook 04)
as confirming robustness. Do not exclude L01 from Provo (it is the most
selected hook there and behaves normally).

## Open questions for supervisor

1. **L01 universality for Provo** — is it theoretically interesting that
   the embedding layer alone predicts eye-tracking, or is it a
   methodological artefact of the 128-token matched context window? The
   argument for it being real: the raw embedding encodes word frequency,
   morphological complexity, and semantic category — exactly the variables
   that predict FFD in the psycholinguistics literature.

2. **Sparse vs dense reversal as main finding** — two framings are possible:
   - "SAE features explain reading time beyond surprisal" (focus on SAE > surprisal gap)
   - "Sparsity helps for lexical processing but hurts for integrative processing"
     (focus on SAE > dense for Provo, dense > SAE for NS)
   The second framing is more mechanistically precise and probably more
   novel, but it requires the reader to accept that the SAE vs dense
   comparison is meaningful. Worth discussing with the supervisor.

3. **Feature 9237 sign reversal** — this is the clearest mechanistic
   result in the paper. Should it be a dedicated subsection or folded
   into the general feature discussion?

4. **Natural Stories bootstrap width** — 10 texts means 10-unit bootstrap.
   Intervals are wide. A participant-level mixed-effects model using
   `processed_RT.tsv` would tighten the NS estimates and is the right
   robustness check for a journal submission.

5. **SRP depth** — we trained a custom SRP (rowEV = 0.80) rather than
   using a pretrained checkpoint because no GPT-2 checkpoint exists in
   the SRP repo. Should we contact the SRP authors to request a GPT-2
   small checkpoint, or is our trained checkpoint sufficient for the
   paper's claims?
